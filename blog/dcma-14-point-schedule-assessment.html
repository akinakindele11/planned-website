The DCMA 14-Point Schedule Assessment, Explained

Published July 2026 8 min read Project Controls

The DCMA 14-point assessment is a quality check that scores a project schedule against 14 metrics (broken logic, excessive lags, hard constraints, negative float and more) to judge whether the programme is trustworthy enough to steer a project by. It was developed by the US Defense Contract Management Agency for defence contracts, but UK and international project controls teams now use it as a fast, standards-based sanity check before a schedule goes out for approval, submission or audit.

What Is the DCMA 14-Point Assessment?

It's a checklist, not a certification. The DCMA published it as part of its guidance on evaluating Integrated Master Schedules on US government contracts, and it has since become a de facto industry standard because it's quick to run, quantifiable, and catches the errors that most commonly wreck a critical path calculation: activities with no logic ties, illogical lags, hard constraints that override the network, and float or duration outliers that suggest the schedule was built in a hurry.

It doesn't check whether your scope is complete, whether your durations are realistic, or whether the plan reflects how the work will actually happen. A schedule can score 100% on all 14 points and still be a poor plan. Treat it as a floor rather than a ceiling: a way of catching structural defects before you get into the harder work of judging whether the logic and durations are actually right.

The 14 Metrics and Their Thresholds

Most Primavera P6 schedules are checked against these standard tolerances. A "pass" on any one metric doesn't mean zero issues. It means the proportion of affected activities sits inside DCMA's tolerance band.

# Metric What it checks Threshold
1LogicActivities missing a predecessor or successor (excluding start/finish milestones)≤5%
2LeadsActivities with negative lag0%
3LagsActivities with positive lag≤5%
4Relationship typesProportion of relationships that are Finish-to-Start≥90%
5Hard constraintsActivities with a "Must Start On" / "Must Finish On" type constraint≤5%
6High floatActivities with total float above 44 working days≤5%
7Negative floatActivities with total float below zero0%
8High durationNon-summary activities running longer than 44 working days≤5%
9Invalid datesActual dates set after the data date, or forecast dates set before it0%
10ResourcesIncomplete activities with no resource or cost assigned0%
11Missed activitiesActivities finishing later than their baseline finish date≤5%
12Critical path testWhether adding a large lag to an activity pushes the project finish out proportionallyPass/fail
13Critical Path Length Index (CPLI)(Critical path length + total float) ÷ critical path length~1.00
14Baseline Execution Index (BEI)Activities completed ÷ activities the baseline says should be complete≥0.95

Some organisations exclude Level of Effort activities and near-term milestones from the logic and duration checks. Confirm which convention your client or contract requires before you run the numbers, because it changes the percentages.

How to Run the Check in P6 and Oracle Primavera Cloud

How you run it depends on your tool. P6 Professional 22.12 and later include a built-in Check Schedule Report (Tools > Check Schedule Report) that scores the schedule against the standard metrics; earlier P6 Professional versions have no built-in report, so you check the metrics manually with layouts (or a third-party tool). P6 Web (EPPM) has the same native Check Schedule report, and Oracle Primavera Cloud (OPC) has an equivalent Schedule Health Score.

  1. Set the data date and reschedule first. Run the scheduler (F9) so total float, dates and the critical path reflect current progress, not a stale calculation.
  2. Run the built-in check if your tool has one. P6 Professional 22.12+ (Tools > Check Schedule Report), P6 Web (EPPM), and Oracle Primavera Cloud (OPC, via the Schedule Health Score) all generate a summary against the standard checks (logic, lags, constraint types, float, duration and BEI) in one pass. On older P6 Professional versions without the report, use the manual layout method below.
  3. On older P6 Professional versions, or where you need a metric the built-in report doesn't cover (CPLI, invalid dates, or a version breakdown by contract package), build a layout with the columns you need (Total Float, Free Float, Constraint Type, Relationship Type, Predecessor/Successor counts), then group and filter to count the exceptions manually. This is slower but it's the only reliable way to see which activities are failing each test, not just the percentage.
  4. Export the flagged activities, not just the summary score. A percentage tells you whether you have a problem; the activity list tells you where it is and who owns it.
  5. Re-run after every remediation pass. Fixing logic frequently creates new float outliers or shifts the critical path, so treat this as iterative, not a one-off gate before submission.

What a Fail Actually Means

This is where most guides stop at the percentage and move on. In practice, a failed metric means different things depending on which one it is:

  • A logic or negative-float fail usually means the critical path calculation can't be trusted. The schedule may show the wrong finish date entirely, and any downstream reporting (SPI, forecast, EOT exposure) built on it is suspect until it's fixed.
  • A constraint or high-duration fail is often a modelling shortcut: someone hard-coded a date instead of building the logic, or left a summary-level activity un-decomposed. It's a quality problem, but usually not a wrong-answer problem, provided the constraint reflects a genuine external date.
  • A CPLI or BEI fail is a performance signal, not a build defect. It's telling you the plan and reality have diverged, which is a project controls conversation rather than a scheduling fix.

The distinction matters because a client or PMO reading a DCMA report at face value can misjudge severity, treating every red cell as equally urgent, when in fact a 6% high-float score is a much smaller problem than a single negative-float chain running through your critical activities.

DCMA Compliance vs a Genuinely Deliverable Schedule

A 100% DCMA pass tells you the schedule is technically sound. It doesn't tell you the durations are realistic, that resourcing is achievable, or that the logic reflects how the work will actually be sequenced on site. On NEC4 contracts in particular, an Accepted Programme needs to satisfy the Project Manager under clause 31.3. DCMA compliance can support that case, but it isn't the test itself, and a schedule that passes every DCMA check can still be rejected if the method statement and resourcing don't stack up. The same applies to schedules submitted for APM-aligned assurance reviews: the 14-point check is a useful first filter, not the whole review.

That's the gap between a schedule that scores well and one that will actually hold up under scrutiny from a client, funder or delay analyst months later. It's usually where a second, experienced pair of eyes earns its keep, particularly on a schedule inherited from another planner or built under time pressure.

When to Get a Professional Health Check

Run a DCMA check before every major submission (baseline acceptance, monthly update, contract close-out) and any time you inherit a schedule you didn't build. If the results are consistently marginal, or you're not sure whether a fail is cosmetic or structural, that's usually the point to bring in a second opinion rather than keep patching metric by metric. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good DCMA score?

There's no single pass mark for the assessment as a whole. Each of the 14 metrics has its own threshold, and a schedule can be "compliant" while still failing one or two individually. In practice, most clients and PMOs want a clean pass on logic, negative float, invalid dates and resources, since those affect whether the critical path can be trusted, and are more tolerant of marginal misses on high-duration or lag counts if there's a documented reason.

Is the DCMA 14-point assessment mandatory on UK projects?

No. It's a US Department of Defense standard, not a UK or NEC requirement. UK contractors and PMOs use it voluntarily because it's a fast, well-documented way to check schedule quality, and some clients now ask for a DCMA-style report alongside NEC4 programme submissions even though the contract doesn't name it explicitly.

Can I run a DCMA check without Primavera P6?

Yes, in principle. The metrics are calculations any scheduling tool can produce if it exports the underlying activity data (float, constraints, relationship types, dates). In practice it's most established in the Oracle ecosystem, thanks to the native checks in P6 Professional and P6 Web (Check Schedule) and Oracle Primavera Cloud (Schedule Health Score), plus the volume of third-party tools built around .xer exports.

What's the difference between CPLI and BEI?

CPLI measures whether the critical path calculation itself is sound: a value close to 1.00 means total float on the critical path is behaving as expected. BEI measures delivery performance against the baseline, counting how many activities have actually finished versus how many the baseline said should have finished by now. One is a schedule-quality check; the other is a progress check.

Does passing all 14 points mean my schedule is good?

Not on its own. It means the schedule is structurally sound enough to trust the critical path calculation, but it doesn't confirm the durations, sequencing or resourcing are realistic. Treat a full pass as the starting point for a sense-check by an experienced planner, not the end of the review.
